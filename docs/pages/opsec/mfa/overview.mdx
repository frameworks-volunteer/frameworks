# Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA / 2FA)

<TagList tags={frontmatter.tags} />
<AttributionList contributors={frontmatter.contributors} />

## Summary

> 🔐 **Key takeaway:** treat MFA as a core control for every critical account. Prefer phishing-resistant methods
> (passkeys/security keys), avoid SMS whenever possible, and design recovery before incidents happen.

This framework centralizes guidance that often appears scattered across platform-specific sections (Discord, Telegram,
X/Twitter, Google, travel OPSEC, and SIM-swap defense). Use this as your baseline policy, then adapt per platform.

## Why this matters

Account takeover remains one of the highest-impact and most common attack paths in Web3 operations. MFA reduces risk,
but only when implemented correctly:

- weak factors (especially SMS) can still be hijacked
- poor recovery paths can bypass strong MFA
- partial rollout (admins only, no comms accounts) leaves critical gaps

## Factor Strength and Selection

Use the strongest method each platform supports:

1. **Passkeys / security keys (FIDO2/WebAuthn)** — preferred when available
2. **Hardware-backed TOTP setup** (or authenticator app with strong device security)
3. **Push-based MFA** (with number matching and anti-prompt-spam controls)
4. **SMS/voice OTP** — fallback only when no better option exists

### Baseline rules

- Disable SMS 2FA whenever an app-based or hardware method exists
- Store backup codes offline (not only in cloud notes)
- Keep at least one backup factor per critical account
- Separate password storage from OTP generation when possible
- Protect the email account used for account recovery with strong MFA too

## Scope: where MFA must be mandatory

MFA should be required for:

- core communication channels (Discord, Telegram, X/Twitter)
- admin and moderation accounts
- email and identity providers (Google Workspace/SSO)
- code and infrastructure control planes (GitHub, cloud, DNS, CI/CD)
- treasury, multisig, and operational finance systems

## Implementation model (team level)

1. **Inventory accounts** and classify by impact (critical/high/normal)
2. **Set allowed methods** by risk tier (e.g., no SMS for critical)
3. **Enroll owners + backups** for each critical account
4. **Test recovery flows** in tabletop exercises
5. **Review quarterly** (factor drift, lost devices, stale admins)

See the practical checklist at [MFA implementation checklist](/opsec/mfa/implementation-checklist).

## Recovery and anti-lockout design

Strong MFA without a recovery plan can stop your own operations.

- Keep emergency recovery procedures documented and access-controlled
- Use break-glass accounts with stricter monitoring and limited use
- Track revoked/lost devices and rotate factors immediately
- Avoid single-person dependency for critical account recovery

## Minimum operating standard

At a minimum, teams should enforce:

- MFA on every critical account
- no SMS for critical accounts unless technically unavoidable
- documented recovery with offline backup codes
- quarterly ownership and method review
- incident playbook for suspected account compromise
